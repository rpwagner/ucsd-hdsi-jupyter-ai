# Week 3 research: where AI software around Jupyter came from

**Evidence cutoff: October 15, 2026, 00:00 UTC (through October 14 UTC). Collected and checked October 15 UTC / October 15 Pacific.**

Research handoff for #23 and [Week 3](agenda.md), not the final Eye article. The strongest supported finding is that **the code's organizational setting and people's prior Jupyter involvement are independent dimensions**. Personal, company and contrib repositories can contain work by longstanding Jupyter contributors and leaders. Software lineage adds a different question: what code or interfaces were adapted, reused or extracted? Later integrations reused components across implementations. None of those observations establishes ecosystem-wide adoption or a universal architectural destination.

## Scope and evidence rules

The population is **12 selected project/component histories**, from December 2022 through the cutoff, with earlier completion software checked as context. The seven current component-map subjects are Jupyter AI, JupyterLite AI, Jupyter Server Documents (JSD), Jupyter Server MCP, the ACP client, persona manager and frontend AI commands. Five contrasting cases add earlier chat/magics and completions plus Datalayer's separate MCP implementation. The [selection record](data/selection.md) gives discovery queries, exclusions and coverage limits. This is a reasoned sample, not a ranked list or a census.

An included effort must explicitly target Jupyter notebook use, chat, completion or AI tool operations, or supply infrastructure deliberately used for those operations. A general-purpose Python model library usable in a notebook does not qualify by itself. External agents and protocols appear only where actual Jupyter integration is evidenced. JSD qualifies as enabling infrastructure; its document-management origin is not relabeled a model interface.

The [inventory](data/inventory.json) and [readable classification table](data/project-classifications.csv) keep the dimensions explicit:

| Dimension | Categories or evidence | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Code's initial organizational setting** (`initial_code_setting`) | Official Jupyter repository; personal repository; company repository; `jupyter-ai-contrib` repository; unresolved | The contributor's membership, seniority or prior community involvement |
| **People's prior Jupyter involvement** (`prior_jupyter_involvement`) | Prior contribution documented; prior history unknown. Documented newcomer and mixed-background categories require affirmative evidence and are not assigned here. | Absence of involvement merely because a search finds nothing; a current governance role at an earlier date |
| **Software lineage** (`software_lineage`) | Row-specific evidence of a Jupyter implementation, external-software adaptation/fork, model/protocol integration, or extraction of existing Jupyter code | That a new package or repository is an independent invention |

Present repository home/status remains a separate historical observation. The people dimension in this snapshot uses attributable Jupyter/IPython PRs **merged before** the effort's first evidence date. That verifies a prior contribution even for people also known as longstanding leaders; it does not reduce their history to one PR or measure its duration or influence. Dated leadership evidence could establish a different kind of prior involvement in a later audit. The [people evidence](data/people-evidence.json) retains the direct contribution witnesses. Origin accounts identify evidenced early roles, not an exhaustive founder list. A positive project-level classification means **at least one** such contributor; another account with unknown history does not make the team a documented mixed-background group.

For example, **JupyterLite AI: personal repository + prior Jupyter contribution documented**. **Datalayer MCP: company repository + prior Jupyter contribution documented**. **Persona Manager: contrib repository + prior Jupyter contribution documented**, with extraction of existing Jupyter AI code recorded separately as software lineage. None is labeled an outside-community contribution because of its repository setting.

The dates below mean earliest defensible evidence **found in this investigation**. A proposal, scaffold, prototype and release are different events. Imported history, repository creation and a template's initial commit are not automatically the start of a Jupyter AI integration. Historical commits can predate repository creation: the ACP implementation history begins before its January 27 repository metadata date. No first-ever claim follows from this sample.

## From prototypes to releases and shared work

The [May 10, 2023 AWS announcement](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/announcing-new-jupyter-contributions-by-aws-to-democratize-generative-ai-and-scale-ml-workloads/) describes a dedicated AWS team contributing to Jupyter and announces Jupyter AI alongside a separate CodeWhisperer extension. Company sponsorship and work inside the Jupyter community coexist. The [August 2 Jupyter launch post](https://blog.jupyter.org/posts/2023/generative-ai-in-jupyter/) announces v1/v2, magic commands and chat, and credits Brian Granger, Andrii Ieroshenko, Piyush Jain, David Qiu and Jason Weill. That is a dated account of builders and roles, not a roster of who originated every component.

The [October 28, 2025 split announcement](https://github.com/jupyterlab/jupyter-ai/issues/1495) names ten subpackages and five coauthors: `ellisonbg`, `Zsailer`, `jtpio`, `3coins`, `dlqqq`. It explicitly describes reorganizing existing Jupyter AI v3 code. New repository/package counts therefore cannot be interpreted as counts of independently invented capabilities or newly arriving communities.

On [February 24, 2026](https://github.com/jupyterlab/jupyter-ai/issues/1531), Qiu describes the team's experience building Jupyternaut and the decision to prioritize integrating external agents through ACP. Treat reliability, cost and user-feedback statements as the maintainers' account, not an independent benchmark or motives inferred from commits. The [v3.0 release record](https://github.com/jupyterlab/jupyter-ai/releases/tag/v3.0.0) then documents shipping ACP agent support, notebook tools via Server MCP and permission UI. **Publication was April 1, 00:03 UTC, still March 31 Pacific**; a promised March 31 release and the UTC metadata are not contradictory. Release descriptions do not establish that every integration was independently tested in this research.

### Two inspectable development traces

**Browser work becomes a shared integration.** Tuloup's 2024 personal Codestral experiment broadens into JupyterLite AI. In 2026, `brichet` [separates agent and extension code, #353](https://github.com/jupyterlite/ai/pull/353), then [introduces a frontend persona, #356](https://github.com/jupyterlite/ai/pull/356), merged July 29. The discussion identifies a temporary Jupyter Chat compatibility blocker rather than claiming the proposal worked immediately. Brichet also has [a Lab PR merged in 2022](https://github.com/jupyterlab/jupyterlab/pull/13037): this is a subsequent integration contributor with prior Jupyter history, not evidence that he authored the 2024 initial commit.

By the cutoff, pinned [Lite persona Python metadata](https://github.com/jupyterlite/ai/blob/370853782a482903102d3c066950a0bf3dbee711/python/jupyternaut-persona/pyproject.toml) requires persona-manager, AI commands and Jupyter Chat components. The [Jupyter AI v3.2 notes](https://github.com/jupyterlab/jupyter-ai/blob/5b23f55d15ba245347013ffe0a238f26b1f4f1e8/docs/source/releases/v3.2.0.md) separately document using the same frontend commands as JupyterLite AI for notebook operations without RTC. These are concrete reuse links, not evidence that the two agents, execution locations or dependency environments are identical. In fact, the pinned persona-manager ranges differ between the two top-level distributions; this is not an installation compatibility test.

**Integration feedback changes dependencies.** In [JSD #239, June 9](https://github.com/jupyter-ai-contrib/jupyter-server-documents/issues/239), `dlqqq` reports incompatibilities with Voilà, nb-cli and collaboration timelines, proposes reducing overrides, and explicitly acknowledges earlier community work, Sailer, and Brochart/QuantStack's Jupyverse cell-execution design. This is a proposal and attribution record, not proof that all proposed changes shipped. The September 3 v3.2 release provides a later concrete consequence: RTC/JSD become optional and notebook tools gain a frontend-command path. It does not prove that #239 alone caused that release decision. Nor does optional JSD mean server-side documents were abandoned: the pinned metapackage retains an `rtc-jsd` extra.

The two MCP names illustrate parallel implementations, not demonstrated convergence: **Datalayer `jupyter-mcp-server`** and **contrib `jupyter-server-mcp`** have different repositories, distributions and dependency records. The former's February 2025 project declaration precedes the latter's August working implementation in the evidence found. No Datalayer-to-contrib code lineage or required dependency is established here. Both bring an external protocol to notebook operations; that software relationship says nothing by itself about the contributors' prior Jupyter involvement.

They are a chronology index, **not a growth rate**, time series of releases, or evidence of a 2025 ecosystem boom. Later package extraction and deliberate selection of Week 3 components inflate recent component counts. Collapsing Jupyter AI and its five selected infrastructure/integration components into one family would reduce this inventory from 12 to seven cases. That sensitivity makes percentages misleading.

## Interpretation and editorial handoff

**Strongest angle: existing contributors make new things in several places.** The personal origins of Jupytee, Codestral/Lite AI and AI Commands, plus Datalayer's established-contributor connection, offer concrete bridges from Week 2's cross-project people evidence to this week's software history. The caution is essential: proving one prior contributor is not classifying an entire team or showing where every idea originated.

**Second angle: integration work has its own history.** Browser extensions, magics, model-backed chat and notebook tools appear through different paths. The Jflam fork, Codeium's credited reuse, the Jupyter AI split and the Lite/Jupyter AI shared tools show distinct kinds of provenance. They should not be collapsed into one application-to-distributed-system narrative. Jupytee is a counterexample to assuming every early experiment became part of Jupyter AI; no such code lineage is established here.

**Useful development sequence for the writer:** one personal Codestral prototype → rename and later persona discussion → merged integration → shared command/tool layer. Pair it with the JSD compatibility feedback and optional-RTC release as the messy integration work. A self-contained article can use a few of these cases; the inventory is evidence support, not an obligation to name twelve projects in 900 words.

Keep Jupyter AI's organizational status dated: the 2023 launch calls it official, while its cutoff README says it is **under incubation as part of JupyterLab**. Neither statement appoints each contrib package an independent official subproject. Notebook machine readability/computational storytelling remains reserved for another episode. No final `the-eye.md`, teaching environment, provider calls or public publication is part of this work.

## Reproduction and review record

See [data/README.md](data/README.md) for schemas, access and collection limits. The publication [source index](data/source-index.json) retains exact REST request URLs, collection timestamps and evidence identifiers; the working archive of larger third-party text snapshots remains private. Discovery-only web snippets are not classification evidence. Searches retain three witness results and totals; this investigation did not perform a fresh contributor census or repair Week 1's missing provenance.

The analysis checks distinct cases/sources, dated prior merge witnesses, source references and cutoff bounds, then generates the summary, chronology CSV, repeated-account table and SVG offline with Python's standard library. It adapts Week 2's small CSV and blue/purple SVG helpers rather than introducing an analysis platform. The SVG was rendered and inspected at its native 1,100-pixel width; labels, counts and caveats fit. No logo was changed. The inventory and source index use public software history only; no student data, private membership evidence or credentials are included. Publication still requires review of the exact selected files.
