## Ideas prepared for the conversation

This series is a salon, not a graded class. Its recurring question is **What is Jupyter - the technology, the people, or both?** Software, standards and infrastructure are one lens; users, maintainers, contributors and governance are another.

The opening agency question asks whether mentoring this group means acting on behalf of Project Jupyter. It is a question about authority and representation, not a legal determination. License permissions, warranty disclaimers, governance and personal initiative should not be treated as the same mechanism.

Rick's workbench metaphor is a place where messy exploration can produce something useful. "Jupyter is for the messy parts" is his paraphrase of Brian Granger's framing, not a verified quotation. The series follows **people + technology -> messy work -> something meaningful emerges**.

## The Eye

[This week's article](the-eye.md) separates software work that adds AI capabilities to Jupyter from decisions about AI-assisted development of Jupyter itself. It links public examples of policy, repository practice and individual views.

The contributor study is an exploratory snapshot, not a community census. Read [the methods and limitations](research.md) before interpreting the counts. [The data guide](data/README.md) explains the incomplete export and why non-bot accounts do not establish human authorship. The overlap classification does not establish anyone's position on AI policy.

## One way to engage this week

**General pattern:** begin by observing how a community makes a decision.

**Jupyter example:** the public [discussion of LLM-assisted contributions](https://github.com/jupyter/governance/issues/337). Its opening explains the question; comments show different experiences. You can read without posting.

**Optional action:** spend about ten minutes on the opening and a few comments. Bring back one question or one thing you noticed. No installation or GitHub account is needed to read it. There is no requirement to complete this, submit a PR, or agree with a particular position.

## A path from use to participation

In [her June 15, 2026 account](https://blog.jupyter.org/posts/2026/becoming-the-new-jupyterhub-and-jupyter-book-community/), Serena Bonaretti describes trying Jupyter Notebook, sharing it in workshops, joining community events and meetings, then applying for a community-manager role. It is an example of relationships and opportunities developing through participation, not a guarantee of employment.
